# Shot Glass Capacity: How to Check mL, fl oz & Fill Levels

A shot glass described as “2 oz” does not, by itself, tell you how much liquid a sample holds or where your intended pour will sit. Before approving a wholesale order, confirm the unit, the capacity convention and the measurement method for the selected glass.

This checklist helps buyers turn a catalog description into a documented sample check. Use it alongside the [Shot Glass Buying Guide for Wholesale Buyers](https://www.glarivoglass.com/blog/shot-glass-buying-guide-for-wholesale-buyers) when comparing products, decoration and packing.

## Quick checklist: what to confirm before sample approval

- **Product reference:** identify the exact glass model and sample revision.
- **Capacity convention:** separate the nominal catalog size, measured brimful capacity and intended fill volume.
- **Units:** record mL and specify US or Imperial fluid ounces if used.
- **Method:** agree the equipment, water conditions and fill endpoint before comparing results.
- **Results:** keep an individual record for each sample, including any repeat measurements.
- **Acceptance:** agree limits, a sampling plan and how borderline results will be handled before approving production.

## 1. Separate nominal size, brimful capacity and intended fill

**Nominal size** is the stated size used to identify or market the product. Ask the supplier what it represents; a rounded product title is not a measurement report.

**Brimful capacity** is the volume held at an agreed rim-level endpoint. The endpoint matters: filling to a defined rim plane and adding water until a raised dome forms can produce different results. Record how the supplier determines “full.”

**Intended fill volume** is the amount you plan to put in the glass for the application. It may leave space below the rim. Confirm that volume with the actual sample, then assess how the filled glass looks and handles in your service or retail setting.

Keep **unused volume in mL** separate from **vertical headspace in mm**. Subtracting intended fill from measured brimful capacity gives a volume difference. It does not give the distance from the liquid surface to the rim. Measure that distance separately, particularly on a tapered glass; half the internal height need not mean half the volume.

## 2. Specify mL and identify the fluid-ounce system

Use mL as the common comparison field in your buying brief. If an offer also uses fluid ounces, spell out the system. “Oz” alone is ambiguous and should not be used as a confirmed volume specification.

The following are rounded unit conversions from the [NIST conversion table](https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-appendix-b-conversion-factors/nist-guide-si-appendix-b8):

- **1 US fl oz:** approximately 29.57 mL; **1 Imperial fl oz:** approximately 28.41 mL.
- **1.5 US fl oz:** approximately 44.36 mL; **1.5 Imperial fl oz:** approximately 42.62 mL.
- **2 US fl oz:** approximately 59.15 mL; **2 Imperial fl oz:** approximately 56.83 mL.

These conversions do not establish the capacity of a product or prescribe a serving size. For example, 45 mL is approximately 1.52 US fl oz, while 60 mL is approximately 2.03 US fl oz. Neither is exactly the same as 1.5 or 2 US fl oz.

Ask the supplier to confirm a statement such as: “The catalog title is nominal; the sample's brimful capacity will be reported in mL using the agreed method.” Keep the result pending until the sample has been checked.

## 3. Agree a sample-check method before measuring

Choose a method that can distinguish the differences relevant to your order. A measuring jug with widely spaced graduations may be useful for an informal check, but it cannot resolve a narrow acceptance limit simply because you write more decimal places on the record.

Prepare clean, dry samples and a stable, level work surface. Identify each sample before filling it. Record the operator, date, water temperature, equipment identification, resolution and calibration or verification status. Use the same setup for comparable samples.

Two possible approaches are a controlled gravimetric check or delivery of a known water volume using suitable verified volumetric equipment. Agree which approach both parties will use. If using a dispenser or volumetric measure, follow its instructions, including whether it is calibrated to contain or to deliver, and its drainage requirements. Avoid treating water transferred out of a glass with residual droplets as an exact reading of what the glass originally contained.

For a simplified gravimetric screening check, weigh the dry empty glass, fill it to the agreed endpoint, keep its exterior dry, and weigh it again. Subtract the empty reading to obtain the indicated water mass. Volume is estimated from water mass divided by water density at the recorded temperature; treating 1 g as exactly 1 mL introduces error.

[NIST's volumetric calibration procedures](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.7383-2019.pdf) describe the more rigorous controls and corrections needed for calibration, including temperature and buoyancy effects. A buyer's simplified screening check does not replace that process. For tight acceptance limits, agree a validated method and its measurement uncertainty with the supplier or a suitable laboratory.

## 4. Check the rim endpoint and intended fill separately

First, document the brimful endpoint. An instruction such as “fill it up” leaves room for different interpretations. Ask for a written method and a clear side-view reference showing the agreed relationship between the liquid surface and the rim. An uneven rim needs an explicitly agreed reference rather than an improvised judgment.

For a practical comparison, avoid a raised water dome above the rim and record any spill or external droplets that could affect a weighing. Repeat the preparation if the fill endpoint was missed. Use the same endpoint definition for all samples.

When reading water in a graduated measuring vessel, follow its specified meniscus convention and viewing position. Water commonly forms a concave meniscus; the lowest point is the reference in the methods described by [NIST's meniscus-reading guidance](https://www.nist.gov/document/gmp-3-reading-meniscus-20190507pdf). That guidance also explains how viewing position and the chosen graduation reference affect the reading. A drinking glass without a graduation still needs its own agreed brimful endpoint.

Next, put the separately measured intended volume into the sample. Photograph it from the side on a level surface and record the distance to the rim if that distance is part of your requirement. Assess carrying and presentation with the actual service arrangement. A still photograph alone cannot establish spill performance.

If artwork includes a decorative line, do not assume that line marks the confirmed volume. Review its position separately through the [Custom Logo Shot Glasses: Artwork & Sample Approval Checklist](https://www.glarivoglass.com/blog/custom-logo-shot-glasses-artwork-sample-approval-checklist).

## 5. Record individual results before deciding acceptance

The following numbers are invented to illustrate the arithmetic. They are not Glarivo product measurements, recommended capacities or a sampling plan.

- **Sample A:** illustrative brimful result of 50 mL.
- **Sample B:** illustrative brimful result of 51 mL.
- **Sample C:** illustrative brimful result of 52 mL.
- **Mean:** (50 + 51 + 52) ÷ 3 = 51 mL.
- **Range:** 52 − 50 = 2 mL.
- **With an illustrative intended fill of 40 mL:** the unused volume would be 10, 11 and 12 mL respectively.

The average does not show whether every sample meets an agreed limit. Nor does a small set of samples establish consistency across a production lot. Keep the individual results and compare them with the acceptance criteria agreed for the order.

Distinguish a repeat measurement of the same glass from measurements of different glasses. If one glass gives inconsistent readings, review the method and equipment before interpreting the difference as manufacturing variation. Agree a sampling plan appropriate to the order, including how samples will be selected; there is no sample count established by this example.

Define the permitted capacity range, the applicable fill convention and a decision rule for results close to a limit. Do not infer a universal tolerance from a catalog label or assume that rounding makes a borderline result acceptable.

## 6. Copy this capacity approval record into your brief

Use one record per selected model, with separate result entries for each sample. Leave unknown fields marked “To complete” rather than guessing.

- **Model and revision:** product reference, drawing revision and sample identifiers.
- **Order context:** intended application, destination, quantity and production lot if available.
- **Catalog statement:** nominal size exactly as supplied, including the original unit.
- **Confirmed unit system:** mL; US fl oz or Imperial fl oz if also required.
- **Capacity definition:** brimful endpoint description and reference photograph.
- **Intended fill:** target volume in mL and any required vertical headspace in mm.
- **Method and equipment:** procedure reference, instrument identification, resolution and verification status.
- **Conditions:** date, operator, water temperature and relevant setup notes.
- **Individual readings:** raw mass or volume readings, conversion basis where applicable, calculated result and repeat readings.
- **Acceptance criteria:** agreed limits, sampling plan and treatment of measurement uncertainty or borderline results.
- **Decision:** approved, rejected or pending; exceptions, reviewer and approval date.
- **Change control:** retained reference sample and changes that require a fresh capacity check.

Keep the record with the approved sample, artwork revision and order specification. If the glass blank or internal profile changes, request a fresh check before relying on the previous result.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 2 oz shot glass exactly 60 mL?

Not as a unit conversion: 2 US fl oz is approximately 59.15 mL, while 2 Imperial fl oz is approximately 56.83 mL. A product labeled “2 oz” still needs confirmation of its unit convention and measured capacity.

### Can I calculate capacity from the outside dimensions?

Outside dimensions do not account for the internal profile, wall thickness or base thickness. Use them for dimensional checks, and confirm internal volume separately on the sample.

### Does a heavier shot glass hold more liquid?

Empty-glass weight does not establish capacity. Additional glass in the base or walls changes weight without necessarily increasing the space available for liquid.

### Can I use a kitchen scale for sample approval?

It may help with a rough screening check if its performance is suitable, but display resolution alone does not establish accuracy. Match verified equipment performance and the measurement method to the agreed acceptance limits.

### What if the supplier's result differs from mine?

Compare the sample reference, unit system, fill endpoint, water conditions and equipment first. Repeat the agreed method on identified samples, retain the raw readings and resolve the difference before signing off the capacity requirement.
