# Shot Glass Sizes Guide for Wholesale Buyers

Choosing a shot glass by appearance alone is risky. Two glasses may both be described as “1.5 oz,” yet differ in measured capacity, usable fill level, rim diameter, base weight, carton quantity, and suitability for decoration. Those differences affect how the glass looks in service, how much it costs to pack and ship, and whether it fits the intended market.

For wholesale buyers, the right question is not simply, “What is the standard shot glass size?” A better question is:

> Which capacity, shape, construction, decoration, and packing specification fits the intended drink, market, customer, and order?

This guide explains how to compare common shot glass sizes from 25 ml to 90 ml, convert fluid ounces correctly, separate glass capacity from legal serving measures, and prepare a more reliable request for quotation.

## Quick answer: what size is a standard shot glass?

There is no single shot glass capacity that is standard in every country or for every use.

In wholesale catalogs, shot glasses commonly appear around 25-35 ml, 40-50 ml, 55-60 ml, and 70-90 ml. However, the nominal capacity printed in a catalog does not automatically define the legal serving quantity in a bar, and it may describe either a working fill or the volume close to the rim.

For reference, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology gives the approximate conversion:

**1 U.S. fluid ounce = 29.57 ml**

That makes 1.5 U.S. fl oz approximately 44.36 ml and 2 U.S. fl oz approximately 59.15 ml. In commercial product naming, those values are often rounded to convenient nominal capacities such as 45 ml and 60 ml.

The United Kingdom provides a useful example of why market requirements must be checked separately. Gin, rum, vodka, and whisky sold by the glass must use either 25 ml or 35 ml measures, or multiples of the chosen measure, subject to the applicable rules. This does not mean every shot glass sold into the UK must have a 25 ml or 35 ml brimful capacity. It means the buyer must understand how the venue will measure and serve the drink and whether approved measuring equipment or markings are required.

## Nominal, brimful, and usable capacity are not the same

Capacity disagreements often begin because the buyer and supplier are discussing different reference points.

### Nominal capacity

Nominal capacity is the commercial size used to identify the product. It may be rounded for easier communication. A glass sold as 2 oz may be described as 57 ml, 60 ml, or another nearby value depending on the source and measuring convention.

### Brimful capacity

Brimful capacity is the volume the glass can hold when filled close to the top edge under a defined test method. It is useful for comparing the physical volume of different models, but it is usually not the practical serving volume.

### Usable or recommended fill

Usable capacity is the amount that can be served while leaving suitable headspace. The required headspace depends on the drink, serving style, decoration, and handling conditions.

Before ordering, request a specification that states:

- nominal capacity;
- measured brimful capacity;
- intended or recommended fill level, if applicable;
- capacity tolerance and the method used to check it;
- whether any line or marking is decorative or intended as a legal measure.

An unmarked drinking glass should not automatically be treated as an approved measuring instrument. The buyer is responsible for checking the rules that apply in the destination market and use environment.

## How to choose the right capacity

### 10-25 ml: sampling and tightly controlled portions

Very small glasses can work for tastings, sample flights, novelty sets, miniature desserts, and markets where small measured pours are common. Their small size creates several practical concerns:

- the logo area may be limited;
- thick bases can reduce the apparent internal volume;
- small diameter changes can noticeably affect the usable capacity;
- pieces may require careful dividers or inner boxes to prevent contact damage.

These glasses should be evaluated from a physical sample, especially when the decoration contains fine text.

### 30-35 ml: compact single-serve formats

This range is appropriate when the target customer wants a restrained pour, a compact bar presentation, or compatibility with a particular serving system. It may also align with regulated measures in some markets, but a glass capacity by itself does not establish legal compliance.

Ask whether the venue uses a separate thimble measure, bottle-mounted measuring device, calibrated line, or another approved method.

### 40-50 ml: versatile mainstream formats

This is a flexible range for hospitality, retail, promotional, and spirits-brand projects. A 45 ml model corresponds approximately to 1.5 U.S. fl oz, while a 50 ml model provides slightly more presentation space and headroom.

For a buyer comparing several 45-50 ml glasses, capacity alone is rarely enough. Compare:

- top diameter and drinking comfort;
- base thickness and perceived weight;
- straight, tapered, flared, or shaped walls;
- total glass weight;
- printable or engravable area;
- pieces per inner box and carton;
- whether the model is machine-made or handmade.

### 55-60 ml: near-two-ounce and two-ounce formats

These glasses are suitable when the buyer wants a more generous pour, a wider tasting glass, or a product marketed around a 2 oz capacity. NIST's conversion places 2 U.S. fl oz at approximately 59.15 ml, so 60 ml is a practical rounded description.

The larger body can offer more decoration space, but it can also increase carton volume and gross weight. Check the complete packing specification rather than comparing unit prices alone.

### 65-75 ml: oversized shots and presentation formats

This range is useful for layered drinks, larger tastings, desserts, souvenir products, and distinctive promotional designs. Buyers should confirm that the target customer genuinely wants an oversized shot glass rather than a compact tumbler.

Pay particular attention to stability. A tall, narrow 70 ml glass and a short, heavy-base 70 ml glass can provide very different serving experiences even though the nominal capacity is identical.

### 90-120 ml: specialty and multi-purpose formats

Large formats can cross into mini cocktail, espresso, dessert, sampler, or gift-glass territory. They may still be marketed as shot glasses, but the product name should not replace a clear use specification.

For these models, confirm:

- intended beverage or food use;
- whether the product must stack;
- whether a spoon, lid, tray, or gift set is involved;
- maximum acceptable carton dimensions and weight;
- expected retail presentation.

## Capacity is only one part of the specification

Two shot glasses with the same capacity may perform differently because of their geometry and construction.

### Top diameter

The top diameter affects drinking comfort, aroma release, decoration placement, and how easily the glasses can be packed. A wider opening may suit tasting and presentation, while a narrow opening can create a taller visual profile.

### Height

Height influences shelf presentation, carton layout, center of gravity, and available decoration area. Confirm whether the product must fit an existing retail box, bar rack, serving tray, or automatic packing line.

### Bottom diameter and base thickness

A broader or heavier base can improve perceived stability and create a premium appearance. It can also increase unit weight and shipping cost. A heavy base should be defined by measurable dimensions or weight, not only by a marketing phrase.

### Rim profile

Ask for close-up sample images and physical samples to evaluate rim smoothness, thickness consistency, and drinking comfort. If the design will carry a fired decal or other decoration near the rim, confirm the required decoration-free area.

### Shape

Straight-sided glasses generally provide an easier area for logos and text. Tapered, curved, boot-shaped, skull-shaped, heart-shaped, and other novelty designs may improve shelf impact but require additional review of mold detail, usable volume, packing protection, and decoration registration.

### Unit weight

Unit weight affects hand feel, carton gross weight, container loading, and shipping cost. It should appear in the approved specification when it is commercially important.

## Material and production method

Many everyday shot glasses are made from soda-lime glass, but a generic material name does not establish every performance characteristic. Heat resistance, dishwasher durability, food-contact documentation, color consistency, and impact performance should be confirmed for the exact product and decoration system.

### Machine-pressed glass

Machine-pressed production can be suitable for solid shapes, textured surfaces, heavy bases, and larger-volume programs. Buyers should review mold seams, surface appearance, dimensional consistency, and the agreed cosmetic standard.

### Machine-blown glass

Machine-blown production can support different wall and shape characteristics. The buyer should compare wall distribution, weight, rim finish, and production consistency using approved samples.

### Handmade glass

Handmade products can provide distinctive shapes and decorative effects, but reasonable variation may be more visible. The purchase specification should define which differences are acceptable rather than relying on the word “handmade” as either a quality promise or an excuse for uncontrolled variation.

Do not infer the manufacturing process from a product photograph. Request confirmation from the supplier and record it in the approved specification.

## Choosing a decoration method

The best decoration method depends on artwork, order quantity, target appearance, expected handling, and the base glass.

### Screen printing

Screen printing can be appropriate for logos, text, and relatively controlled color areas. Confirm the printable area, color reference, registration tolerance, curing process, and durability test.

### Decal decoration

Decals can support more detailed artwork and multi-color designs. Review edge visibility, placement, color consistency, firing compatibility, and the required distance from the rim.

### Spray color

Spray color can create full-body or partial color effects. Confirm the target color reference, transparency, gradient requirements, masking area, surface consistency, and resistance expected under the agreed use and washing conditions.

### Engraving or etching

Engraved effects can create texture and a more permanent visual impression. Ask whether the effect is molded, mechanically engraved, laser engraved, sandblasted, acid-etched, or produced by another method, because the appearance and production requirements differ.

### Metallic and specialty finishes

Gold rims, metallic effects, ion-plated finishes, and similar treatments require exact confirmation of the process and intended use. Do not assume that a decorated item is microwave-safe, dishwasher-safe, or suitable for every food-contact area without product-specific evidence.

For every decoration, approve a physical sample under realistic lighting. Digital mockups are useful for placement but do not prove final color, opacity, registration, texture, or durability.

## Packaging and shipping considerations

Shot glasses are small, but a wholesale order can contain many pieces per carton. A packing statement such as “144 pcs/ctn” is not enough to compare two offers.

Request the complete packing specification:

- pieces per inner box;
- number of inner boxes per carton;
- pieces per export carton;
- divider, sleeve, tissue, foam, or other protection;
- inner-box and master-carton dimensions;
- net and gross carton weight;
- retail color-box requirements;
- barcode, label, and shipping-mark placement;
- agreed packaging test or inspection method;
- pallet or container-loading assumptions, when relevant.

A lower-cost packing method may increase breakage risk or create handling problems for the buyer's warehouse. A premium gift box may improve retail presentation but reduce pieces per carton and increase freight volume. Evaluate packaging as part of the product, not as an afterthought.

## What to include in a wholesale shot glass RFQ

A complete request for quotation makes supplier responses easier to compare.

Include the following information:

1. **Target market** — destination country and sales channel.
2. **Buyer type** — distributor, retailer, bar, hotel, restaurant, spirits brand, event company, or promotional buyer.
3. **Required capacity** — state milliliters and, if relevant, U.S. fluid ounces.
4. **Capacity definition** — nominal, brimful, or required fill line.
5. **Preferred shape** — straight, tapered, heavy-base, tall, mini mug, novelty, or another profile.
6. **Material requirement** — record the requested glass type without assuming unsupported performance.
7. **Decoration** — logo method, number of colors, artwork size, position, and color reference.
8. **Packing** — bulk, inner box, retail color box, gift set, or custom packaging.
9. **Order quantity** — quantity per design and total quantity.
10. **Sample requirement** — existing sample, decorated sample, pre-production sample, or all three.
11. **Compliance documents** — specify the destination-market documents the buyer actually requires.
12. **Delivery requirements** — target date, destination, trade term, and preferred shipping method.

Ask each supplier to identify assumptions and unconfirmed fields. Do not compare quotations as if they describe the same product when the capacity, weight, decoration, or packing definition is different.

## Sample approval checklist

Before confirming production, check the sample against a written specification.

### Dimensions and capacity

- top diameter;
- height;
- bottom diameter;
- unit weight;
- brimful capacity;
- required fill line or working capacity;
- agreed tolerances and test method.

### Visual quality

- rim appearance and smoothness;
- glass clarity and color;
- visible seams;
- bubbles, stones, scratches, distortion, and other defects against the agreed standard;
- base stability on a flat surface;
- consistency between samples.

### Decoration

- artwork version;
- logo size and position;
- color reference;
- registration and orientation;
- edge definition and opacity;
- agreed adhesion or durability testing;
- required decoration-free area near the rim.

### Packaging

- correct quantity per inner box and carton;
- divider and contact protection;
- retail-box appearance;
- barcode and shipping marks;
- carton dimensions and weight;
- agreed handling or transport test.

Record the approved sample, drawing, artwork, color reference, and packing specification with revision numbers. If any one of these changes, confirm whether a new sample approval is required.

## A practical selection process

Use this sequence to narrow a large catalog efficiently:

1. Define the destination market and use case.
2. Select the capacity band.
3. Confirm nominal, brimful, and working-fill definitions.
4. Choose the shape and base style.
5. Shortlist products that meet the dimensional requirements.
6. Select the decoration process.
7. Define the packing format.
8. Compare complete quotations using the same assumptions.
9. Approve physical samples and specifications.
10. Confirm the production inspection plan before mass production.

This process is more reliable than selecting the cheapest product with a familiar ounce label.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many milliliters are in a 1 oz shot glass?

One U.S. fluid ounce is approximately 29.57 ml, so a 1 oz product is commonly rounded to 30 ml. Confirm the supplier's measured capacity because the commercial label may be nominal.

### How many milliliters are in a 1.5 oz shot glass?

One and a half U.S. fluid ounces equal approximately 44.36 ml. Many catalogs describe this size as 45 ml, but some models may use a nearby nominal value.

### Is a 2 oz shot glass exactly 60 ml?

Two U.S. fluid ounces equal approximately 59.15 ml. “60 ml” is a practical rounded description, not an exact conversion.

### Are 25 ml and 35 ml shot glasses required in the UK?

UK rules specify 25 ml or 35 ml measures, or multiples of the selected measure, for gin, rum, vodka, and whisky sold by the glass. Buyers should confirm the complete measuring and marking requirements instead of assuming that any glass with a similar capacity is an approved measure.

### Is brimful capacity the same as the serving size?

No. Brimful capacity measures the physical volume near the rim. The practical serving size normally leaves headspace and may also be controlled by local rules or separate measuring equipment.

### Which shot glass size is best for custom logos?

There is no universal best size. A straight-sided 45-60 ml glass often provides more usable decoration area than a very small or highly curved model, but the artwork and process should be tested on the exact sample.

### Can decorated shot glasses go in a dishwasher?

That depends on the glass, decoration material, application process, curing or firing conditions, and the agreed test method. Request evidence for the exact decorated product rather than relying on a general claim.

### What is the minimum order quantity for custom shot glasses?

MOQ varies by existing stock, glass model, decoration process, number of colors, packaging, and production setup. Send a complete RFQ and request a confirmed MOQ for the exact configuration. Do not assume that one product's MOQ applies to another.
